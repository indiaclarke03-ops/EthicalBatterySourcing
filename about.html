About this tool

Battery Passport Audit is an ethical-sourcing audit of the EU battery passport. It builds illustrative passports for six battery chemistries, then traces each supply chain from mine to deployment to show what the passport would and wouldn't reveal about human rights, labour, communities and the environment.

Why it exists

The passport promises transparency. Under the EU Batteries Regulation (Reg. 2023/1542, Art. 77), a digital battery passport becomes mandatory on 18 February 2027 for EV batteries, light-means-of-transport batteries and industrial batteries above 2 kWh. The Commission's guidance defines 71 data points.

But the ethical risks sit upstream, where passport data is weakest. None of the 71 data points records where materials were mined or refined. The supply-chain due-diligence duty that is meant to give sourcing data meaning was postponed to 18 August 2027 (Reg. 2025/1561), and it covers only four raw materials: cobalt, natural graphite, lithium and nickel.

Changing chemistry moves risk; it doesn't remove it. Newer chemistries can look "cleaner" on paper because their risky inputs (phosphate, manganese, vanadium, copper, sulphuric acid) aren't on the due-diligence list. This tool puts those inputs back in view.

What it covers

ChemistryPassportWhy it's here

Full passports are filled from research on named companies. A shadow passport is what the research could fill in where too little is public, or where no official passport would exist. The reference passport (NMC) is a lighter baseline: the chemistry the EU due-diligence list was written around.

How it works

  1. Research. Each chemistry starts from India Clarke's earlier Electrum research and is re-verified against primary and public sources, with corporate-registry and trade records from Sayari. Every finding goes into a research note with its source, evidence tier and date.
  2. Scoring. Every value, supply-chain link and risk gets a confidence score (High, Medium, Low or Unknown) and a one-line reason, using one rubric for every chemistry. Risks are labelled by signal type, so a database match is never presented as a finding of harm.
  3. Data. Findings are stored as structured data files: passports, supply-chain nodes and links, sources and assurance schemes. An automated validator checks every file against a schema and the project's rules (for example, that every value has a source and that database forced-labour flags stay aggregate) before anything is published.
  4. Site. These pages read the data files directly in your browser. There is no server, no tracking and no external calls; every number on screen is computed from the same data you can inspect in the repository.

How to use it

Passports
Pick a chemistry and a use case. The card shows what the official passport would say; the panel beside it shows what it wouldn't. Select any field for its sources and the ethical-sourcing risks along that chain. "Show gaps only" narrows the card to the weakest fields.
Scorecard
Chemistry × supply-chain stage. Each cell shows the strongest risk signal, whether EU due diligence would ask about it, and whether any independent assurance exists. Select a cell for the evidence.
Supply chain
Each chain from ore (S0) to deployment (S5). Filter by risk category, switch on the ownership & capital overlay to see who owns and funds what, or the policy risk overlay to see export controls, US lists and other exposures with their dates.
Compare
The six chemistries side by side: which minerals carry risks, where supply is concentrated, which uses the passport covers, how far back each chain could be traced, and how many gaps remain.
Methods
The full rubric, the ethical-sourcing method, how database results are checked, and how AI was used.

Every view has a shareable link: the address bar updates as you choose a chemistry, filter or field.

How to read it

  • High Medium Low Unknown How confident the research is in a value, with the reason one click away.
  • T1 T2 T3 Evidence tier: primary source, credible secondary source, or weaker source.
  • ● Documented harm ◆ Screening flag ▲ Structural risk ○ No known evidence What kind of signal a risk is. A screening flag is not an allegation, and "no known evidence" is not "no risk".
  • dashed A stage or field that couldn't be traced from public or licensed data. Gaps are shown, never filled in.
  • Side-by-side values mean sources disagree. They're never averaged.

Limits

  • The passports are illustrative. They're not real product passports and make no statement about any company's compliance.
  • The data is a snapshot as of . Policy items carry their own dates; export controls and EU rules are still moving.
  • A blank or "no known evidence" result reflects what the research could find, not a clean bill of health.
  • Nothing here is legal, compliance or investment advice.
